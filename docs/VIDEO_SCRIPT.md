# If you're asked to just explain it

No screen, no clicking. Two minutes.

> Animals arrive at a shelter every day — found on the street, or given up by
> owners who can't keep them. The shelter takes them all, but it has a limited
> number of kennels, a limited number of people willing to foster, and a small
> budget for helping animals get noticed.
>
> I looked at eleven years of real data — a hundred and seventy-one thousand
> animals — and found something that surprised me. **Four out of every hundred
> stay longer than three months, and those four take up a third of the entire
> shelter.**
>
> A very small group uses most of the space. And when a shelter fills up,
> everything gets worse for every animal in it.
>
> The real problem is that on day one, nobody knows which ones they'll be. So the
> extra help — a foster home, a photo on the website, a waived fee — goes to
> whoever is easiest to notice. The friendly dog at the front. The kitten
> everyone stops for.
>
> The animals who need it most are the quiet ones nobody notices, until they've
> already spent ninety days in a cage.
>
> That's what this is for. A staff member types in what they already know on the
> first day, and the tool gives back the chance that this animal is still here in
> three months — and explains why.
>
> **For the animal**, help arrives on day one instead of day ninety. Three months
> in a kennel, or three months in a home.
>
> **For the staff**, the hardest decision of the morning gets a starting point.
> They're already making it — from memory and instinct, on very little sleep.
>
> It doesn't decide anything and it doesn't replace anyone's judgement. It says:
> look at these ones first.
>
> And it's honest about what it doesn't know. For cats it's barely better than
> guessing, so it says so out loud instead of pretending. A shelter worker
> trusting a confident number that means nothing is worse off than one with no
> number at all.

## One line

Longstay is a triage tool for shelter staff: on the day an animal arrives, it
shows who is at risk of staying for months, so the limited help goes to them.

## Short — paste this under the video

> **Longstay** — intake-day triage for animal shelters.
>
> A shelter has a fixed number of kennels, a fixed number of foster homes, and a
> fixed amount of staff attention. Some animals go home within a week, some stay
> for months — but on day one nobody knows which is which. So help goes to
> whoever is easiest to notice rather than whoever needs it most.
>
> Longstay takes what is already known on the day of intake — species, breed,
> age, colour, how the animal arrived, its condition — and estimates the
> probability that it will still be here in three months. Today's intakes are
> ranked by that probability, and every estimate explains itself: which facts
> pushed it up, which pushed it down.
>
> It is built on real data from the Austin Animal Center, Texas — the city's two
> open datasets, every intake and every outcome, **171,561 animals over 11.6
> years**. The model learns from 2013–2022, is tuned on 2023, and is tested on
> 2024–2025, which it has never seen. The split is by date, never random:
> shuffling would let the model see the future and the score would mean nothing.
>
> A decision tool, not a dashboard. It decides nothing — it says who to look at
> first.

## Longer — if you need more than a caption

**Why.** Not every day in a shelter costs the same. **4.2% of animals stay
longer than three months, and they account for 33.1% of all the time the shelter
has to give** — measured in animal-days, because a kennel held for 152 days is
152 days of capacity however you count the animal in it. Another 54.4% leave
within a week and use only 7.5%. A unit of attention spent on the first group is
worth far more than the same unit spent on the second.

**What it computes.** The probability that the stay will run to 90 days or more.
That is the headline number and the queue is sorted by it. An estimate in days is
shown too — in smaller type, next to a caveat — because it honestly does not
work: every regression variant scores a negative R² (−0.116), meaning it predicts
worse than a constant. That is not an unfinished piece of work, it is the
finding: length of stay is largely not explained by what is knowable at intake.

**How it helps.** For the animal, the difference between three months in a kennel
and three months in a foster home is settled on day one, not on day ninety. For
staff, the hardest decision of the morning gets a starting point instead of
instinct: a worklist ordered by risk, with each row explained. There is a batch
mode — upload a CSV of the day's intakes and get it back sorted.

**How accurate.** 2.4× better than random selection by PR-AUC. At the operating
threshold it flags roughly one intake in ten, and of a hundred flagged animals
about fourteen really do wait three months, where random selection would find
five. For dogs it is 2.7× better than random; for cats only 1.6×, which is below
the confidence floor, so on cats the interface says so instead of showing a
confident number.

**What it cannot see.** Photographs, temperament, behavioural notes, medical
complexity beyond the coarse condition label, staff effort and volunteer
advocacy, local adoption events, kennel visibility. All of it decides an animal's
fate and none of it is in the data. That is why Longstay sorts a queue rather
than delivering a verdict.

# Background — for questions, not for reading aloud

**Where the data comes from.** Austin publishes two open files: every animal that
arrived, every animal that left. Eleven years, about 174,000 rows each.

**Why matching them is hard.** Animals come back — adopted, returned, admitted
again. Same ID, several visits. So each arrival is paired with the *next*
departure for that animal, and one departure can only close one arrival. Tests
cover this, because an error here would corrupt every number silently.
**Result: 171,561 clean stays.** Dropped: 1,586 still in the shelter, 88 broken
records, 577 who stayed over a year.

**What's measured.** `length of stay = date they left − date they arrived`. Then
the concentration, in animal-days rather than head count, because a kennel held
for 150 days is 150 days of capacity:

| Group | Share of animals | Share of days |
|---|---:|---:|
| Leave within a week | 54% | **7%** |
| Stay 90+ days | 4% | **33%** |

**Why the split is by date.** Shuffling randomly lets the model learn from 2025
and be tested on 2019 — it sees the future, and the score is meaningless. So:
learn 2013–2022, tune on 2023, test on 2024–2025. Lower score, real one.

**What the model learned by itself.** Pit Bulls wait longer than Chihuahuas.
Adult dogs wait longer than puppies — in the tail, not the average. Cats admitted
in May wait twice as long as cats admitted in January. Injured animals mostly
leave fast, but a few get stuck for months.

**What happens when you click.** Your words become numbers ("3 years" → 1,095
days), the model answers, the app checks whether it's reliable for that species,
then works out which inputs moved the number by swapping each to "typical" and
asking again. About 100 milliseconds.

**Likely questions.**

*Why is accuracy low?* — Because the things that decide an animal's fate aren't
in the data: the photograph, the temperament, whether one volunteer fights for
it. 2.4× better than random is the real signal that exists.

*Why delete a feature that helped?* — Because it wasn't knowable on day one.
Keeping it would make the numbers look better and be false.

*Is 14 out of 100 useful?* — Yes. Right now attention goes to whoever is most
visible. Three times better than random, on day one, beats that.

*Why predict duration and not the outcome — adopted, returned, transferred?* —
Everyone who takes this dataset predicts the outcome. But on day one a shelter
does not need to know how it ends, it needs to know how long. Capacity is spent
in days, not in outcome categories.

*Why is the test period 2024–2025 specifically?* — We predict the future from the
past, so the test set has to sit strictly later than the training set. A random
shuffle would let the model learn from 2025 and be scored on 2019.
